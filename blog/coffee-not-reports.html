The RM 13,500 Lesson: Why a Coffee Beat My Market Research Report

Mikael Chew · July 1, 2026 · 7 min read

The best market research I ever did cost me RM 13,500. Only RM 7.50 of it was money well spent.

I was launching a new anti-ageing cream. I wanted to do it properly, so I bought a 200-page market research report for RM 3,500. It told me exactly who to target: urban women aged 25 to 35 who care about how they look.

So I did what the report said. I put RM 10,000 into Facebook ads aimed at exactly that group. Three months later the numbers came in: plenty of impressions, very few enquiries, two customers and RM 800 in sales.

I was down RM 12,700 on a professional report and precise targeting. It was the worst result I had ever produced.

The coffee

A few days later I was sitting in a café with an old friend, a woman in her forties who had been my customer for years. She could see I was in a bad mood and asked what was wrong.

I told her about the cream. Her eyes lit up. "I want that. My mother is 75 and her skin is so dry. I've been looking for something good for her."

Then she said the sentence that changed my whole campaign. "At our age, what worries us isn't getting old ourselves. It's watching our parents get old."

The report had told me what: which age group buys anti-ageing products. One coffee told me why. The real buyers were people aged 45 to 60, buying for their ageing parents.

Twenty more coffees

One conversation could have been a fluke, so I put the report away and tested it. Over the next 30 days I had coffee with 20 existing customers and asked the same few questions.

  • Why did you buy?
  • What were you really trying to solve?
  • What nearly stopped you buying?
  • If you bought an anti-ageing cream, who would it be for?

Fifteen of the twenty said some version of the same thing. "For my mum." "My dad's skin is getting so dry." "My parents are older now and I want them to be comfortable."

My direction had been completely wrong. The report described who should buy. My customers told me who did.

Same product, same budget, a different story

I rebuilt the campaign around what those twenty people said. The old ads spoke to young women about staying young. The new ones spoke to grown children about helping their parents age with comfort and dignity.

The picture changed too. Out went the young model. In came a middle-aged daughter gently putting cream on her elderly mother's hands.

Same product. Same ad budget. Within a month, enquiries had multiplied several times over, and sales were more than ten times what the old campaign produced. The only thing that changed was that I finally knew who my customer was.

If you know the enemy and know yourself, you need not fear the result of a hundred battles. — Sun Tzu

What Sun Tzu meant by knowing the other side

Everyone in this industry has heard that line. Very few act on it. Most people think knowing the other side means collecting information: reports, statistics, competitor price lists.

Information tells you what is happening. It rarely tells you why. And in direct selling, the why is where every decision gets made, because people buy for reasons they feel long before they can explain them.

You can't download that from a report. You have to sit across the table from a real person and listen long enough for them to tell you.

How to run your own coffee research this month

You don't need a budget for this. You need a list and the patience to ask before you sell. Here is the exact process I used.

  • Pick ten people who already buy from you. Start with customers rather than prospects, because they have already made the decision you are trying to understand.
  • Ask for their opinion, not a sale. "I'm trying to understand my customers better. Can I buy you a coffee and ask you a few questions?" Almost nobody says no.
  • Ask about the reason, then stay quiet. "Why did you buy?" is where it starts. The real answer usually comes after the first answer, when you let the silence sit.
  • Write down their exact words. Their phrasing becomes your marketing language later, so don't tidy it up.
  • Look for the pattern, not the outlier. One person is a story. Seven out of ten is a market.

Most people who do this find at least one thing that surprises them. Sometimes it's who is buying. Sometimes it's the reason. Often it's the objection they never heard, because nobody told them to their face.

The lesson I paid for so you don't have to

I'm not against research. Numbers help. But a report can only describe the people its authors imagined. Your customers are real, they are already in your phone, and most of them would be glad to talk to you.

Knowing the other side takes two things no report can sell you: the humility to ask, and the discipline to listen before you decide.

So here's my question for you this week. When did you last ask a customer why they bought, and actually listen to the whole answer?
