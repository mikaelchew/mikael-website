Teach One Thing at a Time: Why Your Best Training Is Your Shortest

Mikael Chew · September 30, 2026 · 6 min read

Most of us have run a training like this. Three hours, forty slides, everything we know about prospecting, follow-up, product knowledge and mindset. People take notes and clap at the end.

A week later, ask what changed. Usually very little. The notes are in a drawer and the habits are the same as before.

The training felt generous. It just tried to do too much at once.

People change one habit at a time

Information is easy to give and hard to use. Most people can only adopt one new behaviour at a time, and only if they practise it soon after learning it.

A training with ten ideas usually produces zero new habits, because nobody knows which one to start with. A training with one idea, practised properly, can change how a whole team works.

Regard your soldiers as your children, and they will follow you into the deepest valleys. — Sun Tzu

Sun Tzu wrote about caring for soldiers as you would your own children. Good parents don't teach a child everything in an afternoon. They teach one thing, watch it tried, correct it gently, and come back to it until it sticks.

The one-skill format

Here is the structure I like for team trainings. It fits into 45 minutes and works online or in a room.

  • One skill, named clearly. "Opening a conversation by asking for an opinion." Not "communication". The narrower the better.
  • Why it matters, in five minutes. One reason, one example, no more.
  • A live demonstration. Do it in front of them, ideally for real. Watching it done is worth more than any slide.
  • Practice in pairs. Twenty minutes of people actually trying it, swapping roles, getting it wrong and trying again.
  • One commitment. Everyone leaves with a specific number: "I'll use this opener with three people before Friday."

Check it next week

The part most trainings skip is the follow-up. Start the next session with five minutes on last week's skill. Who used it, what happened, what got in the way.

Those five minutes do two things. They show everyone that training leads to action here, and they give you real stories from your own team to teach with.

Why short training duplicates

There's one more advantage. A forty-slide training can only be run by the person who built it. A one-skill session can be run by any partner who has practised it a few times.

That's how training spreads through a team without you in every room. Your partners can teach one skill to their own people next week, and they will.

So look at your next training plan. If you had to cut it down to one skill, which would you keep?
