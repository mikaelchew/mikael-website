Speak From Experience, Not From the Brochure

Mikael Chew · July 15, 2026 · 6 min read

There are two ways to talk about a product. You can repeat what the brochure says, or you can describe what happened when you used it.

The first sounds polished and forgettable. The second sounds a little rough, and people remember it.

Most people can tell which one they are hearing within a few seconds. That difference decides whether they trust you.

Know your equipment

Sun Tzu's famous line is about knowing the enemy and knowing yourself. Elsewhere he spends a lot of time on method: organisation, discipline, and the equipment an army carries into the field.

Method and discipline: the marshalling of the army, the gradations of rank, and the control of supplies. — Sun Tzu

In direct selling, your products are part of that equipment. Reading the brochure gives you a shallow knowledge of them. The deep kind comes from living with a product until you can talk about it without notes.

The 30-day log

My rule is simple. Before I share a new product, I use it myself for at least 30 days, even when my team is impatient to start.

During those days I keep a short log. It's tedious, and that tedium is where the value comes from.

  • Use it at a fixed time every day, so it doesn't depend on remembering.
  • Write one line a day: what you used, when, and anything you noticed.
  • Record what didn't change just as carefully as what did.
  • Note the practical things too: taste, routine, cost, whether it fits a busy week.

That third point matters most. A log that only records good news is an advertisement. A log that records the gaps too is evidence.

How the conversation changes

After 30 days you stop sounding like a salesperson. You sound like someone with notes.

Instead of "Everyone loves this," you can say something closer to this: "Here's what I noticed over a month, and roughly when. This part didn't change much for me, so if that's your main reason, it may not be the right fit. Your body may respond differently to mine, so try it and watch for yourself."

Notice what that does. You have told them something negative, and their trust goes up rather than down. People believe the person who admits a limit far more readily than the one who claims everything is perfect.

Three lines you should never cross

Speaking from experience comes with responsibilities. Honest sharing only works if you stay honest about where your experience ends.

  • Never promise a result. Say what happened for you. Let the other person decide what it means for them.
  • Never play doctor. If someone has a medical condition or takes medication, ask them to speak to their doctor before they start anything.
  • Never borrow someone else's story as your own. If a customer shares their experience, ask permission before you repeat it, and say clearly that it was theirs.

Why it's worth the wait

Thirty days feels slow when your upline is excited and the launch is this week. It is still the best investment you can make in your own credibility.

A rushed month of sharing reaches a few people who half believe you. A month of honest experience lets you speak with confidence for years, and the people you share with can feel it.

So be honest with yourself on this one. Is there a product you share today that you couldn't talk about from your own experience?
