STRATEGIC LEADERSHIP

One Sheet of Paper: The Honest List That Broke a Leader's Plateau

Mikael Chew · July 8, 2026 · 7 min read

Joanne was doing everything right. She trained well, spoke well, and worked harder than most leaders I knew. Her team still wouldn't move.

For years it sat somewhere between 150 and 200 people. New partners came in. About as many drifted out. Leaders who started around the same time as her had moved up a level or two, and she was still in the same place.

One day she came to me, frustrated, and asked what she was doing wrong. I told her the truth. "It isn't effort. You don't know yourself well enough yet."

Two columns

I took out a sheet of paper and drew a line down the middle. Strengths on the left, weaknesses on the right. I asked her to fill it in and be completely honest.

The left column took her about a minute. Public speaking. Training. Motivating people. Strategic thinking.

Then I said, "Now the right side. That's the one that matters." She went quiet for a while before she started writing. One-to-one follow-up. Keeping track of details. Managing numbers. Planning money.

When she finished she looked at the page for a long time. It wasn't comfortable to read.

Your team copies all of you

I pointed at the right-hand column. "Your gift is standing on a stage and lifting a hundred people. Your gap is sitting down with one person and staying with them."

Then I asked her a harder question. "How many of the leaders you've built are exactly like you? Great on stage, and nowhere to be found for the follow-up?"

She didn't answer, because she already knew. Her team had copied her strengths, and it had copied her weaknesses just as faithfully.

The result was a team that was excellent at attracting people and poor at keeping them. They were stuck in a loop: bring people in, lose them, bring more in, lose them too.

If you know the enemy and know yourself, you need not fear the result of a hundred battles. — Sun Tzu

Most people read that line and think about the enemy. The harder half is know yourself. Most of us know our strengths very well. Our blind spots are blind for a reason.

Stop patching. Build a system.

The usual advice at this point is to work on your weaknesses. Go on a course, force yourself to love spreadsheets, become someone you're not. I've watched leaders spend years on that and come out exhausted and only slightly better.

We did something different with Joanne. We kept her strengths where they were and built a system around the gaps.

First, take memory out of follow-up. She was never going to remember every new partner's day four. So we stopped relying on her memory. Every new partner and every prospect went into Google Calendar with reminders on Day 1, Day 4, Day 7, Day 14 and Day 30, each with a short checklist of what to say and do.

The calendar remembers so she doesn't have to. Her weakness stopped dragging her strengths down with it.

Second, find the people who are strong where you are weak. Joanne started deliberately looking for detail people. Partners who naturally love one-to-one care, who track numbers without being asked, who make sure nobody falls through the cracks.

She stood on the stage and trained. They sat down and stayed with people. Neither side had to pretend to be the other.

Third, spend most of your time where you are strongest. About 80% of her week went back to what she did best: training, speaking, strategy. The rest was covered by the calendar, a helper for the admin, and the partners who loved the detail.

What changed

Six months later her team had grown, and far more new partners were still active after their first few months. Twelve months later she broke through to the next rank.

A year later she was no more talented than before. What had changed was her honesty about the gaps, and the system she built to cover them.

Do this tonight

Get a sheet of paper. Draw the line. Write your strengths on the left, then spend twice as long on the right.

  • Write the weaknesses you would rather not admit, not the safe ones you use in job interviews.
  • Next to each one, write which of three fixes it needs: a system, a helper, or a partner whose strength it is.
  • Then look at your team and ask which of those weaknesses they have already copied.

That last step is the uncomfortable one. It's also where most plateaus are hiding.

So here's the question. If your team copied you exactly as you are today, which column would hold them back?
