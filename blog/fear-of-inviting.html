The Real Reason You Avoid Inviting, and How to Stop Avoiding It

Mikael Chew · August 12, 2026 · 6 min read

Ask a struggling builder how many people they invited last month and the answer is usually small. Five. Eight. Maybe ten in a good month.

Ask why, and you'll hear about being busy, waiting for the right moment, or not wanting to bother people. Those are real, but they are rarely the full story.

Underneath most of them sits one quiet feeling: I don't want to hear no.

What you are actually afraid of

It helps to be precise. Hardly anyone fears the word itself. People fear what they think the word means.

  • That the other person now thinks less of them.
  • That a friendship will become awkward.
  • That a run of noes proves they aren't cut out for this.

Write your version down. Fears look smaller on paper than they feel at night, and once you can name one you can start to deal with it.

Victorious warriors win first and then go to war. — Sun Tzu

Win before you pick up the phone

Sun Tzu said the victorious army wins first and then seeks battle. For an invitation, that means settling the outcome in your own mind before you make the call, so whatever happens in the call can't knock you over.

Here is the simplest way to do that. Every invitation has only three possible answers: yes, no, or maybe. There is no fourth one.

Decide in advance what you'll do with each. A yes gets a time and a place. A no gets a warm thank-you and no pressure. A maybe gets a date in your calendar to check in again. When every answer already has a next step, none of them is a disaster.

Four ways to make it lighter

  • Shrink the ask. You aren't asking someone to change their life. You're asking whether they'd like to come to a talk, look at something, or give you their view. Small asks get honest answers.
  • Count invitations, not results. Set a daily number you control, such as three invitations, and judge your day by that. Results follow volume over weeks, not hours.
  • Separate the person from the answer. A no is information about their timing and needs. It says nothing about your worth, and very little about whether they like you.
  • Protect the friendship out loud. Say it plainly: "If it's not for you, that's completely fine, and it changes nothing between us." Then mean it.

The cost of not asking

Avoiding invitations feels safe, but it has a cost you don't see on the day. Every person you don't invite is a person who never got to decide for themselves.

Some of them would have said yes. You'll never know which ones, because you made the decision for them.

Respecting people means letting them choose. A clear, kind invitation with no pressure is one of the most respectful things you can offer someone.

So who on your list have you already said no for, without ever asking them?
