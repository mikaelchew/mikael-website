Busy for 60 Days, Zero Customers: The Seven Daily Actions That Fixed It

Mikael Chew · July 22, 2026 · 8 min read

I see this story so often I could tell it with my eyes closed.

A new partner joins. He works hard. He posts on social media every day, goes to every training, reads every product brochure twice. Sixty days later he has no customers and is ready to quit.

I ask him what he actually did each day, and he gives me a long list. Then I ask how much of it involved talking to a real person. He works it out and says, "Maybe 20%?"

That's the whole problem. Eighty percent of his effort went into getting ready, and twenty percent into the activity that produces results. He was busy in all the wrong places.

The general who wins makes many calculations in his temple before the battle is fought. — Sun Tzu

Sun Tzu prized careful planning, and in the very next chapter he warned that long campaigns drain every resource. His answer was to plan well and then move quickly. In this business that means a clear goal and a short daily list you can actually finish.

The Daily Method of Operation

DMO stands for Daily Method of Operation. These are the seven actions I did every day as a distributor, and the same seven I have taught ever since. Do them for 90 days and you will see your business start to look different.

A good DMO has three qualities. It's simple enough to start today. Every item can be counted, so you know each night whether you did it. And it's never broken: the power comes from 90 days without a gap, not from one heroic day.

  • Use the product every day and write one line about it. Belief comes from experience. When someone asks if it really works, you can look them in the eye and say you use it every day.
  • Send your upline a three-line report. What you did today, where you got stuck, what you'll do tomorrow. Your upline is there to save you from detours, so tell them when you're stuck.
  • Check in on one partner. Ask how they are, how the week's goal is going, and how you can help. If you have no partners yet, check in on a customer instead.
  • Meet three to five new people. Meet them as people, without pitching. Online, comment on their posts and start real conversations. Offline, talk to people at events, classes and cafés. Write each one down that night.
  • Share something of value with five people. A short post, a story, an article that would help them. Keep it about 80% value and 20% product, and send some of it one-to-one.
  • Follow up with five people you've already spoken to. Most lost opportunities were never rejected; someone simply forgot to follow up. Put every follow-up in your calendar so it doesn't depend on memory.
  • Invite three people to an event. Keep it short and let the event do the heavy lifting. "There's a sharing session this Saturday I think you'd enjoy. Want to come along?"

How to fit it into a day with a full-time job

Most people building this on the side have one or two hours a day, at best. Here's a pattern that works for many of my partners.

  • Morning, about 15 minutes: use the product and log it, then check today's follow-ups in your calendar.
  • Lunch, about 20 minutes: post one useful thing and send a few one-to-one messages.
  • Evening, 45 to 60 minutes: invitations, one partner check-in, write down the people you met, and report to your upline.

The exact times don't matter. What matters is that each of the seven has a fixed slot, so you never start the evening wondering what to do.

When seven is too many

Very few people manage all seven from day one, especially with a job and a family. If your time is genuinely tight, protect three: use the product, meet new people, and follow up. Those three create results directly.

Then build up. Three actions for the first week, five from the second, and all seven once you have 30 days behind you.

And when life gets in the way, as it will, don't start again from zero. A plan's job is to show you how far you've drifted. Adjust from where you are today.

If a month passes and nothing happens

Before you blame the system, check the quality. Are the people you're meeting the people you actually want to serve? Is what you share genuinely useful to them? When you follow up, are you showing care or applying pressure?

Usually the volume is fine and the direction needs a small adjustment. The DMO is a tool, and it works best with the right strategy behind it.

Write the seven actions on a card tonight and put it where you'll see it tomorrow morning. Then answer this honestly: of the hours you put into this business last week, how many were spent with actual people?
